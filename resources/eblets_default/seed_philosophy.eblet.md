## Facts

**Q: What is the mind-body problem in philosophy?**
A: The mind-body problem asks how mental states (consciousness, thoughts) relate to physical brain states. Dualism (Descartes) holds they are separate; physicalism holds mental states are physical brain states; functionalism focuses on functional roles.

**Q: What is utilitarianism?**
A: Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill) holds that the morally right action is the one that produces the greatest total happiness for the greatest number. It is consequentialist: outcomes determine moral worth.

**Q: What is Kant's categorical imperative?**
A: Kant's categorical imperative is a universal moral law: "Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." Moral duties are absolute, not dependent on consequences.

**Q: What is epistemology?**
A: Epistemology is the branch of philosophy studying knowledge: what it is, how we acquire it, and its limits. Key debates: rationalism (knowledge from reason) vs. empiricism (knowledge from experience); foundationalism vs. coherentism.

**Q: What is Plato's theory of Forms?**
A: Plato held that the physical world is a shadow of a higher realm of eternal, perfect Forms. The Form of the Good is the highest; physical objects are imperfect instances. Knowledge is recollection of these Forms.

**Q: What is existentialism?**
A: Existentialism (Sartre, Camus, Kierkegaard, Heidegger) holds that existence precedes essence: people are not born with predetermined purpose but must create their own meaning through choices and actions in an indifferent universe.

**Q: What is the trolley problem?**
A: The trolley problem is an ethical thought experiment: should you divert a runaway trolley to kill one person rather than five? It tests consequentialist vs. deontological intuitions and whether action/inaction matters morally.

**Q: What is empiricism vs. rationalism in epistemology?**
A: Empiricists (Locke, Hume, Berkeley) hold that knowledge comes primarily from sensory experience. Rationalists (Descartes, Leibniz, Spinoza) hold that reason is the primary source of knowledge, independent of experience.

**Q: What is the problem of free will?**
A: The free will problem asks whether human choices are determined by prior causes (determinism) or undetermined (libertarian free will). Compatibilism holds that free will and determinism are compatible.

**Q: What is Aristotle's virtue ethics?**
A: Aristotle's virtue ethics holds that moral goodness consists in developing excellent character traits (virtues like courage, justice, temperance) through habit. Virtues lie between extremes (the golden mean); eudaimonia (flourishing) is the goal.

**Q: What is the social contract theory?**
A: Social contract theory (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau) explains political authority as arising from a voluntary agreement. People give up some freedoms to a governing authority in exchange for security, rights, and order.

**Q: What is the philosophical problem of induction?**
A: Hume's problem of induction: past observations cannot logically guarantee future regularities. We cannot prove the sun will rise tomorrow just because it always has. This challenges the foundations of empirical science.

**Q: What is Hegel's dialectic?**
A: Hegel's dialectic describes historical and conceptual development through contradiction: a thesis is opposed by an antithesis, and the tension resolves in a synthesis that preserves elements of both while transcending them.

**Q: What is moral relativism vs. moral realism?**
A: Moral relativism holds that moral judgments are true only relative to cultural or individual frameworks. Moral realism holds that there are objective moral facts true independent of any person's beliefs.

**Q: What is phenomenology?**
A: Phenomenology (Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty) is the philosophical study of conscious experience from the first-person perspective. It examines how things appear to consciousness, prior to theoretical frameworks.

**Q: What is the concept of justice in political philosophy?**
A: Rawls' theory of justice states that principles should be chosen behind a "veil of ignorance." His difference principle allows inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged members of society.

**Q: What is analytic philosophy?**
A: Analytic philosophy (Russell, Wittgenstein, Frege) emphasizes logical analysis of language and arguments. It contrasts with Continental philosophy in its focus on precision, argument structure, and philosophy of language.

**Q: What is the paradox of the heap (sorites)?**
A: The sorites paradox: if you remove one grain from a heap of sand, it remains a heap. But removing grains one by one eventually leaves no heap. It raises questions about vagueness and sharp boundaries in concepts.

**Q: What is Descartes' method of doubt?**
A: Descartes used radical skepticism to find indubitable truths. He doubted everything until reaching "cogito ergo sum" (I think therefore I am) — the only thing he could not doubt was that he was doing the doubting.

**Q: What is the trolley problem vs. the doctrine of double effect?**
A: The doctrine of double effect permits harmful actions if the harm is a foreseen but unintended side effect of achieving a good end. It differs from utilitarian calculation by considering intention, explaining different moral intuitions about similar outcomes.
